A medical equipment specification has two jobs: to make sure the hospital gets equipment that does what its clinicians need, and to allow a fair comparison between the products offered. Specifications copied from a single manufacturer's brochure fail the second test — and in public procurement they invite challenge.

## Begin with the clinical requirement

Before writing a single parameter, define what the equipment must achieve: the procedures it supports, the patient groups it serves, the department it sits in and the throughput expected. A specification for an ICU ventilator, for example, should flow from the ventilation modes and patient categories the unit will actually manage.

## Specify performance, not features

Good specifications describe measurable performance and acceptable ranges rather than proprietary features or trade names. Where a particular capability is genuinely essential, describe the outcome it delivers so that equivalent approaches can qualify.

Useful practices include:

- **Use ranges, not single values**, unless a precise figure is clinically justified.
- **Separate essential from desirable** requirements, so evaluation can weigh them appropriately.
- **Avoid unique dimensions or terms** that only one product can match.

## Cover the whole life of the equipment

Purchase price is only part of the cost. Specifications should also address installation requirements, user and technician training, warranty, availability of spares and consumables, service response, calibration needs and the expected support period. These are often where offers differ most.

## Align with the site

Equipment that arrives before the room is ready — or that the room cannot support — delays commissioning. Power, medical gas, water, drainage, structural loading and shielding requirements should be checked against the facility design as specifications are written.

## Make evaluation transparent

A defensible technical evaluation uses a compliance statement that maps every bid line-by-line against the specification, supported by documentary evidence. When the specification is written around outcomes, this comparison is straightforward, and the decision can be explained to any committee or auditor.
