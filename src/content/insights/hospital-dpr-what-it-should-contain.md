A Detailed Project Report is the document a promoter, board or funding agency uses to decide whether a healthcare project goes ahead. For government projects it also becomes the reference against which tenders are written and technical bids are evaluated. A DPR that is thin in the wrong places costs months.

## Start from the clinical brief, not the building

Every number in a DPR should trace back to a clinical decision. Bed count, the mix of general, private and critical-care beds, the number of operation theatres, diagnostic modalities and outpatient capacity together define the facility. When those decisions are explicit, reviewers can follow the logic from service to space to cost. When they are implicit, every downstream figure invites a query.

A strong DPR therefore opens with:

- **The service definition** — specialities, catchment and expected patient load.
- **Departmental briefs** — what each department does, its operating hours and its key adjacencies.
- **The schedule of accommodation** — every room, its area and the department it belongs to.

## Show the engineering scope, not just an allowance

Engineering services in a hospital — HVAC, medical gases, electrical distribution with standby power, water treatment and sewage treatment — represent a large share of capital cost and almost all of the operational risk. A lump-sum "MEP allowance" tells a reviewer nothing.

Instead, the DPR should describe each system at concept level: the HVAC approach for critical areas, the MGPS source equipment and outlet schedule, the electrical load estimate with backup philosophy, and water and effluent capacities. This is the basis on which cost estimates become defensible.

## Plan equipment by department

Medical equipment is frequently the largest single budget line. An equipment plan organised by department — with quantities, broad technical categories and budgetary costs — lets reviewers check that what is being bought matches the services being offered. It also becomes the starting point for vendor-neutral specifications later.

## Make the cost estimate traceable

The cost section should be built bottom-up from the schedule of accommodation, the engineering scope and the equipment plan, with clearly stated rates and assumptions. Contingency, escalation and pre-operative costs should appear as separate lines rather than being buried in unit rates.

## Include the approvals roadmap and programme

Statutory approvals and licences shape the schedule as much as construction does. A realistic programme that sequences planning, approvals, procurement, construction, commissioning and handover — with the critical path identified — shows that the project team understands what it is committing to.

## A DPR is a design document

The most useful DPRs are written to be built from. When the same team that prepares the report goes on to engineer, equip and commission the facility, the departmental briefs, equipment lists and cost framework carry straight through — and the project starts its next stage without re-work.
